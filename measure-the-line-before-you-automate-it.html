Measure the line before you automate it: four lessons from industrial AI research

Counting, weighing, inspecting and planning: what four studies on real production problems say about where AI pays off on the factory floor.

By Dr. Robert Kerwin C. Billones · October 8, 2026

Advanced Manufacturing and RoboticsIndustrial AI and Data

When manufacturers ask me where to start with AI, they usually expect an answer about algorithms. My answer is almost always about measurement. Most production problems I see, in factories and in food processing plants, come down to the same gap: the plant does not have reliable, timely numbers on what it produces, what it rejects and where work piles up. Until that gap closes, smarter software has little to work with.

Four studies I co-authored with colleagues and students at De La Salle University’s Department of Manufacturing Engineering and Management and its Intelligent Systems Laboratory show this from different angles. Each one tackles a single job on a production line:

  • Counting. Replacing photoelectric sensors with cameras to count dehusked coconuts on a conveyor at a coconut by-product plant.
  • Weighing. Estimating the weight of mangoes from conveyor video, without a scale, for postharvest sorting and packing.
  • Inspecting. Detecting and classifying ten types of defects on metal surfaces with deep learning.
  • Planning. Simulating an 11-machine production line to find and fix the bottlenecks that kept it from meeting demand.

Read together, they describe a sequence: sense the line, check its output, model it, then decide. Here is what each step taught us.

1. Cameras can upgrade the sensors you already have

Many plants in developing economies cannot rebuild their facilities to adopt new technology, so they settle for cheaper sensing that is good enough. The coconut plant we worked with counted product with photoelectric sensors that were about 88% accurate, limited by their short range and narrow detection angle.

We replaced them with an ordinary camera and an object detection and tracking pipeline (YOLOv8 with ByteTrack). The detector reached 97.9% average precision, and on three test videos the system counted between 91.8% and 97.8% of the coconuts that passed. That is a meaningful improvement from equipment that costs little and mounts above an existing conveyor.

The mango study pushed the same idea further: using a camera to replace an instrument, not just a sensor. From video of fruit moving along a conveyor, the model extracted four simple features, including length, width and pixel counts, and estimated each mango’s weight. Its estimates explained 98.5% of the variation in actual weight, with an average error of about 2.6%. For a postharvest line, that means grading by weight without stopping the fruit.

The lesson: before buying new instruments, ask whether a camera and a well-trained model can measure what you need from the line as it already runs.

2. The data sets the ceiling

The defect study is a useful warning. Using a public dataset of metal surface images, we trained a detector to find and label ten kinds of defects, from weld lines and oil spots to creases and rolled pits. The model was good at saying what a defect was. It was much weaker at drawing a box around where it was.

The cause was not the algorithm. Some defect types had far fewer examples than others, and some images contained obvious defects that had never been labeled, so the model was penalized for finding them. The coconut study hit the physical version of the same problem: it traced its counting errors to low-resolution frames and cluttered backgrounds, and the fix it recommends is better cameras and higher frame rates rather than a different model.

The lesson: budget for data before you budget for models. Balanced examples, complete labels, good lighting and adequate camera resolution set the upper limit of what any AI system on the line can achieve.

3. Lab accuracy is not line accuracy

Two of the studies show a clear gap between how well a model performs on its narrow task and how well the system performs on the job. In the defect study, classification was strong while detection lagged. In the coconut study, the detector scored 97.9%, but counting in live video ranged from 91.8% to 97.8%, because counting depends on tracking each coconut across frames as well as recognizing it.

The lesson: measure the outcome the plant actually cares about, such as the daily count, the weight grade or the reject rate, under real line conditions. Model metrics from a test set are a starting point, not a commissioning result.

4. Simulate before you buy machines

The fourth study moves from sensing to deciding. An auto-component foundry’s core shop in India ran an 11-step process on 11 machines and could not meet its average demand of 24,300 cores a month, even after earlier process improvements. We modeled the line as a cyber-physical system in MATLAB Simulink and ran repeated rounds of bottleneck analysis: find the slowest operation, add capacity there, simulate again.

By the sixth configuration, the simulated line met monthly demand within 27 working days, with a buffer of more than 1,100 cores for unexpected rejects. Four more rounds improved how evenly the machines were used. The study is also candid about the limits. Adding machines takes money and floor space the plant may not have, and overtime or outsourcing may meet demand more cheaply. The model’s value is that it lets managers compare those options before spending on any of them.

The lesson: treat a simulation or digital twin as a decision tool. Its job is to show which investment actually moves output, and which ones only move the bottleneck somewhere else.

How the pieces fit together

These studies were separate projects, but they belong to one system. A bottleneck model is only as good as the production counts, cycle times and reject rates fed into it. Cameras that count, weigh and inspect on the line are what supply those numbers continuously, instead of from manual logs. Put the two together and you have the foundation of a working digital twin: a model of the line that stays current because the line itself keeps it updated.

The studies also connect in a less obvious way. In the core shop model, inspection, at 60 seconds per unit, was the second-slowest step, and the bottleneck kept moving between curing and inspection as machines were added. Faster automated inspection, like the defect detection in the third study, is one way to relieve that kind of constraint without adding another inspection station. Sensing and planning are not separate projects: better measurement can change where the bottleneck sits.

This matters especially for Philippine manufacturers. The World Economic Forum’s 2018 readiness assessment placed the Philippines among “legacy” countries for the future of production. Closing that gap will not require every plant to rebuild. It will require plants to measure what they already do, accurately and automatically, and to use those measurements to make better decisions.

A practical sequence for manufacturers

  1. Pick one number that matters. Output count, weight grade, defect rate or throughput at a known bottleneck.
  2. Audit the current measurement. How is it captured today, how accurate is it, and how late does it arrive?
  3. Pilot camera-based sensing on the existing line. Fix lighting, camera position and resolution before tuning the model.
  4. Invest in the data. Balance examples across defect or product types and label every image completely.
  5. Validate on the line. Compare the system’s count or grade against a manual baseline over full shifts, not test images.
  6. Feed the data into a model of the line. Use simulation to test capacity, staffing and investment options before committing capital.

AI on the factory floor rarely fails because the algorithm is too weak. It fails when it is asked to make decisions from data the plant never measured well. Start with measurement, and the rest of the roadmap becomes much easier to build.
